Did renewables actually displace fossil power — or just ride on top of it?

The world's renewable electricity share rose from 18.7% to 29.5% this century. But fossil generation kept growing too. This page lets you see, country by country, whether the clean-energy transition is really breaking the link between growth and carbon — or just adding a greener line to a still-expanding system.

3Decoupling is spreading — slowly

The Tapio index classifies each country over a window: absolute decoupling (CO₂ falls while GDP rises) is the good kind. The share of countries achieving it rose from 11% in 2000–05 to 34% by 2017–22.

4Where renewables cut emissions

A two-way fixed-effects model (country + year) estimates the % change in CO₂ per capita per +1 percentage-point of renewable share. The effect is large and significant in Europe and the Americas; weaker and uncertain in Asia and Africa.

Splitting countries into terciles by their 2000 renewable share sharpens the mechanism: the emissions dividend is strongest where grids were already clean (top tercile, 2000 share >48.8%) and near zero where systems were just starting out (bottom tercile, ≤6.5%).

5But did they displace fossil? Mostly, no.

Between 2000 and 2022 the world added 5,636 TWh of renewable generation and 7,861 TWh of fossil generation. Only 51 of 163 countries (31%) actually cut their fossil output. The correlation between a country's renewable-share gain and its change in fossil generation is essentially zero (r = 0.00): gaining renewable share tells you almost nothing about whether a country burned less coal and gas.

Each point is a country: x = change in fossil generation (TWh), y = change in renewable generation (TWh). Points left of zero (green) displaced fossil; almost everything sits right of zero — fossil rose. Europe is the only major region that displaced fossil (ratio +0.34, 71% of its countries cut fossil); Africa (−2.54) and Asia (−2.29) added fossil far faster.

6Where this lands by 2030

Extrapolating the last decade's momentum, the global renewable share reaches about 36% by 2030 (95% interval 35–37%). Real progress — and still far short of fossil's ~61% share.

7Fossil generation keeps rising to 2030

Hold the last decade's momentum of renewable share, low-carbon share and electricity demand, and fossil generation does not bend down — it climbs. The build-out has been additive, not substitutive.

At current momentum, global fossil generation rises from 17,693 TWh to about 19,345 TWh by 2030 (an added 1,652 TWh).
What it would take instead: just to hold fossil generation flat to 2030, the low-carbon share must reach 49.5% — but constant momentum only delivers 44.5% (a 5.0-point shortfall). Cutting fossil 30% needs 64.7%. Displacement has to be engineered, not assumed from the share.
