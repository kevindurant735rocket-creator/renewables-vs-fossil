#Where this is heading: fossil keeps rising to 2030

Hold the 2013–2022 momentum of renewable share, low-carbon share and electricity demand, and fossil generation does not bend down — it keeps climbing.

At current momentum, global fossil generation rises from 17,693 TWh (2022) to about 19,345 TWh by 2030 — an added 1,652 TWh. The transition is adding clean power faster than it is retiring dirty power.

#Country stories: who displaced, who piled on

Six countries, six very different trajectories. The clean-energy leaders (Germany, Denmark, the UK, the US) cut fossil generation; the fast-growers (China, India) added enormous amounts even as their renewable share rose.

#Myth vs fact

The findings get misread in predictable ways. Here are the four most common.

“Renewable share is rising everywhere — so we’re winning.”

Share and absolute generation are different things. Because total electricity demand grew, the world added 5,636 TWh of renewables and 7,861 TWh of fossil over 2000–2022. A rising renewable share mostly means clean power is catching up to a growing total — not that coal and gas are being switched off.

“Lower CO₂ per capita proves the transition is working.”

It proves the intensity link is real — each +1pp of renewable share is associated with about −0.61% CO₂/capita. But intensity falling is not the same as fossil generation falling. The fixed-effects result is an associational dividend, not evidence that fossil plants were retired.

“We’re just early — it’ll flip soon.”

Not at current pace. Holding the last decade’s momentum, fossil generation reaches about 19,345 TWh by 2030 — up from 17,693 TWh in 2022. Decoupling is spreading (absolute-decoupling countries rose from 11% to 34%), but absolute fossil output is still growing.

“So this is anti-renewables?”

No. Renewables are clearly cutting emissions per person, strongly in Europe and the Americas. The point is narrower and more useful: the build-out has been additive, not substitutive. Closing fossil plant — not just building renewables — is the missing half of the transition.

#How to — read, reproduce, cite

The study is built to be checked. Three short guides below.

The one-sentence finding: renewable share is linked to lower CO₂ per capita almost everywhere (about −0.61% per +1pp), yet the growth is overwhelmingly additive — fossil generation rose by 7,861 TWh while renewables rose by 5,636 TWh, and only 31% of countries cut fossil output.

How to read the results

1

Decoupling ≠ displacement

“Lower emissions per person” (a fixed-effects intensity result) is not the same as “less fossil power.” The displacement question is about absolute generation, and there the picture flips: globally, fossil grew more than renewables did.

2

Where it works

The emissions dividend is largest in the Americas (−0.76%/pp) and Europe (−0.53%/pp), and weakest where grids were just starting out. Split by 2000 baseline, it is strongest exactly where renewable penetration was already high.

3

Why 2030 still matters

Extrapolating recent momentum gives roughly 36% renewable share by 2030 — real progress, still far below fossil’s ~61% share.
